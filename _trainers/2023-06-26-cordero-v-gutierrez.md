The consolidated proceedings involve petitioners Joel Cordero, Carolina Ramos, Estrella Reyes (represented herein by her heir Napoleon Damayo, Jr., a.k.a. ## Briefly summarize the one-sentence disposition the Supreme Court reached in these petitions.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court dismissed the petition in G.R. No. 205074 as moot and academic because the main case before the Court of Appeals had already been resolved on the merits, and it denied the petition in G.R. No. 231518 for lack of merit, affirming the Court of Appeals' rulings that the RTC did not commit grave abuse of discretion in issuing orders that led to the issuance of a writ of execution enforcing a final and executory judgment in favor of Gutierrez Development Co., Inc.
</p>
</details>

## What are the core facts concerning the subject land, the relationship of the parties, and the rent arrangement?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Respondent Gutierrez Development Co., Inc. was the registered owner of a parcel of land located in Poblacion, Davao City, evidenced by Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-52804. Petitioners had been occupying the subject land for many years; initially their occupancy was by tolerance, and later they were required to pay a token monthly rental of ₱100.00 each. When respondent acquired the property, it continued the same arrangement, but alleged that petitioners neglected their obligation to pay the token rentals. These longstanding facts gave rise to respondent's Petition for fixing the period of lease and adjustment of lease rentals before the RTC.
</p>
</details>

## What specific relief did respondent request in its Lease Petition filed in the RTC?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
In its Lease Petition, respondent sought a judicial fixation of the remaining period of the petitioners' lease and an adjustment of lease rentals. More precisely, respondent prayed that the period of the remaining lease be fixed for not longer than one year and that the rental be set at a rate commensurate with the then-current value of the subject land. Respondent also prayed for such other reliefs as may be just and equitable under the circumstances. The petition was thus a judicial attempt to set a definite period and reasonable rent, and, implicitly, to regain possession at the end of the fixed period.
</p>
</details>

## How did petitioners respond legally to the Lease Petition, and what counterclaim did they assert?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Petitioners answered the Lease Petition by asserting defenses and factual averments. They contended they engaged in negotiations with respondent regarding extension of their leases, or alternatively, arrangements for payment for improvements they had introduced on the lot in the event they had to vacate. Those negotiations, however, did not materialize. Substantively, petitioners filed a counterclaim seeking the aggregate value of their introduced improvements in the amount of ₱46,500,000.00, and also prayed for moral damages and attorney's fees. Thus, petitioners not only resisted respondent's petition to limit the lease period and adjust rent but also sought compensation for their asserted investments in the property.
</p>
</details>

## What did the RTC rule on the Lease Petition and what reasoning did it provide?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Regional Trial Court rendered a Decision dated August 31, 2006, granting respondent's petition. The RTC fixed a lease period of two (2) years for petitioners' occupancy of the property, at the same rental of ₱100.00 per month for each petitioner, reckoned from the date of the RTC decision. The RTC explained its reasoning by noting that petitioners had occupied the premises for a very long time (even prior to 1976, according to petitioners' own claim), and that such indefinite occupancy could not be allowed to continue. The court emphasized the lessor's right to enjoyment of its property and cited precedents to the effect that the law should not unduly deprive a lessor of possession simply because the lessee had been loyal. The RTC therefore balanced the interests of both parties and concluded that a two-year fixed period was just and equitable. The decision did not pronounce as to costs and liabilities.
</p>
</details>

## How did the Court of Appeals rule on petitioners' appeal from the RTC decision (CA-G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN), and what was its key adjustment concerning the two-year period?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court of Appeals, in a Decision dated September 25, 2008, affirmed the RTC's ruling as to its substantive resolution but made a key temporal point: the CA held that the two-year period fixed by the RTC decision began on August 26, 2006 (the CA indicated reckoning from the date of the trial court's decision). By the time of the CA's resolution, that two-year period had already lapsed. Consequently, the CA held the contract of lease to be terminated, ordered that petitioners turn over possession of the leased property to respondent, and dismissed the appeal. The CA thus affirmed the RTC's fixation of the period but recognized that the fixed term had already expired by the time of its ruling, making immediate termination and turnover appropriate.
</p>
</details>

## What post-judgment procedural actions did respondent take to enforce the appellate judgment, and what did the RTC do in response?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
After the Court of Appeals' Decision became final and executory (the CA denied the petitioners' motion for reconsideration by a Resolution dated April 3, 2009, and the Entry of Judgment was later issued), respondent filed a Motion for Execution with the RTC on June 23, 2009, seeking issuance of a writ of execution to enforce the CA ruling in CA-G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN. The RTC granted the motion in an Order dated September 18, 2009, and issued the Writ of Execution on September 28, 2009. Petitioners filed a Motion for Reconsideration with the RTC arguing that the two-year period should have been reckoned from the finality of the CA ruling (i.e., from October 25, 2008), but the RTC denied their reconsideration motion on May 15, 2010. Thus, the RTC directed execution consistent with the final appellate determination.
</p>
</details>

## What contention did petitioners raise in their motion for reconsideration to the RTC about when the two-year period should be computed?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Petitioners contended in their Motion for Reconsideration that the two-year period fixed by the RTC's decision should not be reckoned from the date of the RTC ruling itself, but instead should be computed from the finality of the Court of Appeals' ruling. They argued that the two-year period should start on the date the appellate ruling became final—identified in the record as October 25, 2008—so that execution should await the expiration of that computed two-year period. The RTC, however, denied this motion, and the issue ultimately was addressed by the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court in the subsequent certiorari proceedings.
</p>
</details>

## What extraordinary remedy did petitioners pursue after the RTC issued the writ of execution, and how did the Court of Appeals first treat their request for injunctive relief in CA-G.R. SP No. 03631-MIN?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
After the RTC issued the writ of execution and denied reconsideration, petitioners filed a Rule 65 petition for certiorari before the Court of Appeals (docketed as CA-G.R. SP No. 03631-MIN), asking the appellate court to correct the RTC's actions and praying, inter alia, for the issuance of a temporary restraining order (TRO) and/or writ of preliminary injunction (WPI) to halt enforcement. The Court of Appeals, in a Resolution dated December 29, 2011, denied the prayer for a TRO and/or WPI, reasoning that a thorough examination of the assailed RTC Orders vis-à-vis the petition's averments showed no urgent reason to issue injunctive relief. The CA directed the parties to submit memoranda thereafter and ultimately denied the Rule 65 petition on the merits in a later Decision dated June 22, 2016.
</p>
</details>

## Explain why the Court of Appeals initially concluded there was "no urgent reason" to issue injunctive relief to petitioners.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court of Appeals considered the Rule 65 petition's factual and legal averments alongside the RTC orders under assault and determined that petitioners did not establish the extraordinary circumstances necessary to warrant issuance of a TRO or WPI. The CA observed that the RTC Orders were issued in accord with a final, executory appellate ruling (CA-G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN), and that the execution of a final judgment is governed by settled rules. Because the writ of execution sought to effectuate a judgment that had already attained finality and thus was immutable, there was no pressing or exceptional basis to restrain enforcement; petitioners had not shown the urgency or likelihood of grave abuse needed to justify injunctive relief. This led the CA to deny the prayer for TRO/WPI and to proceed to decide the Rule 65 petition on the merits.
</p>
</details>

## What are the two distinct issues the Supreme Court identified for resolution in these consolidated petitions?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court framed two discrete issues: (a) In G.R. No. 205074, whether the Court of Appeals correctly denied petitioners' prayer for a temporary restraining order (TRO) and/or writ of preliminary injunction (WPI) in CA‑G.R. SP No. 03631-MIN; and (b) In G.R. No. 231518, whether the Court of Appeals correctly found that the Regional Trial Court did not commit grave abuse of discretion when it issued the orders that resulted in the issuance of a writ of execution in favor of respondent pursuant to the Court of Appeals' ruling in CA‑G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN. Each issue called for examination of procedural propriety and the proper reach of extraordinary remedies.
</p>
</details>

## Why did the Supreme Court dismiss G.R. No. 205074 as moot and academic?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court dismissed G.R. No. 205074 as moot and academic because the relief sought in that petition — the propriety of the Court of Appeals' denial of a TRO/WPI — was an incident of the main case (CA‑G.R. SP No. 03631-MIN) and, by the time of the petition to the Supreme Court, the main matter before the Court of Appeals had already been resolved on the merits. The Court invoked settled doctrine that an issue becomes moot and academic when supervening events eliminate any justiciable controversy such that judicial adjudication would have no practical effect. Here, since the principal litigation had been finally resolved by the Court of Appeals on the merits, any determination about the earlier denial of temporary injunctive relief would be purely academic, offering no substantial relief or practical consequence. Consequently, the petition had to be dismissed.
</p>
</details>

## State the legal test for "moot and academic" questions as articulated in this case.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court restated the pertinent test: a case or issue is moot and academic when it ceases to present a justiciable controversy by virtue of supervening events, so that adjudication would be of no practical value or use. In such circumstances there is no actual substantial relief the petitioner would be entitled to that would be negated by dismissal, and courts generally decline jurisdiction because the judgment would serve no useful practical purpose or legal effect. In short, when the resolution of the principal controversy renders an ancillary claim purely academic, courts will dismiss related petitions to avoid issuing advisory or futile rulings.
</p>
</details>

## What was the principal issue in G.R. No. 231518, and what standard of review governs petitions for certiorari under Rule 65 attacking judicial acts?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
In G.R. No. 231518, the principal issue was whether the Court of Appeals correctly found that the RTC did not commit grave abuse of discretion when it issued orders directing a writ of execution to enforce the final CA judgment. The standard of review for a Rule 65 petition is exceedingly narrow: the petitioner must show "grave abuse of discretion" by the lower court. The Supreme Court reiterated the definition of that term, explaining that grave abuse occurs when a court's act is a capricious or whimsical exercise of judgment equivalent to a lack of jurisdiction, such that it is patent and gross — tantamount to a virtual refusal to perform a duty or an arbitrary exercise of power. In other words, certiorari is reserved for truly extraordinary cases where the lower court's action is wholly void or exercised in a palpably arbitrary manner.
</p>
</details>

## How does the Court define "grave abuse of discretion," and what does this definition require a petitioner to demonstrate?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court, citing Chua v. People, defined "grave abuse of discretion" as an act of a court or tribunal done in a capricious or whimsical exercise of judgment equivalent to lack of jurisdiction. The abuse must be so patent and gross as to amount to an evasion of a positive duty or a virtual refusal to perform a legal duty, or an arbitrary and despotic exercise of power born of passion or hostility. This exacting definition means the petitioner must manifestly show that the lower court's action was not merely erroneous or improvident but was a grossly unreasonable exercise of judgment that effectively nullifies the court's jurisdictional duty — a very high threshold to clear in a Rule 65 petition.
</p>
</details>

## How did the Supreme Court apply the "grave abuse" standard to the RTC's issuance of orders leading to execution?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Applying the grave abuse standard, the Supreme Court concluded that the RTC did not commit grave abuse of discretion. The RTC's issuance of the writ of execution was preceded by a final and executory Court of Appeals judgment (CA‑G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN) which had become immutable. Given the doctrine that final judgments, once executory, are immutable and that execution of such judgments is a matter of right, the RTC's acts were ministerial and in compliance with its duty to enforce the appellate ruling. The petitioners failed to demonstrate any capricious, arbitrary, or legally void exercise of authority by the RTC; instead, the RTC's orders strictly implemented an existing final judgment. Thus, the stringent showing required for grave abuse was not met.
</p>
</details>

## Explain the doctrine of immutability of final judgments as articulated by the Court in this decision.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The doctrine of immutability holds that a decision that has acquired finality becomes immutable and unalterable; it may no longer be modified, even to correct errors of fact or law, whether by the court that rendered it or by the Supreme Court. The Court explained the doctrine serves two-fold purposes: procedurally, to avoid delays in the administration of justice by making orderly the discharge of judicial business; and substantively, to put an end to judicial controversies so that parties' rights and obligations do not hang indefinitely in suspense. The doctrine recognizes the societal and institutional interest in finality, accepting at the risk of occasional errors that litigation must come to an end. In this case, the CA ruling had attained finality and thus was immutable, making the RTC's enforcement action unassailable under the standards of Rule 65.
</p>
</details>

## What practical consequences follow from the immutability of final judgments in relation to execution?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Once a judgment becomes final and executory, it cannot be altered and execution of that judgment becomes a matter of right for the prevailing party. Practically, this means the trial court's role in issuing the writ of execution is ministerial: upon motion, the court must issue the writ to enforce the judgment once the period to appeal has expired or no appeal has been perfected. The Court emphasized that allowing litigants to frustrate execution by belated collateral remedies subverts the function of the courts and denies the winner the fruits of victory. Therefore, execution upon a final judgment is to be promptly effectuated to give practical effect to the final adjudication.
</p>
</details>

## What Rule of Court provision governs execution upon judgments, and how did the Court invoke it in its decision?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court invoked Rule 39, Section 1 of the Rules of Court, which provides that execution shall issue as a matter of right, on motion, upon a judgment or order that disposes of the action or proceeding upon the expiration of the period to appeal therefrom if no appeal has been duly perfected. The Supreme Court used this provision to reinforce the point that, because the Court of Appeals' ruling had become final and executory, the issuance of a writ of execution in favor of respondent was procedurally proper and mandatory—the trial court's action to issue the writ was not discretionary but ministerial once the prerequisites for execution had been met.
</p>
</details>

## How did the Court of Appeals' finality and Entry of Judgment affect petitioners' subsequent attempts to block execution?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court of Appeals' decision in CA‑G.R. CV No. 00991-MIN became final and executory after denial of motion for reconsideration (resolved April 3, 2009) and the issuance of the Entry of Judgment. That finality rendered the appellate ruling immutable and entitled the prevailing party to execute the judgment as a matter of right. Petitioners' subsequent attempts—such as seeking reconsideration in the trial court and later filing a Rule 65 petition with prayers for TRO/WPI—were evaluated against the backdrop of that finality. Because execution was procedurally proper and the lower courts acted to enforce an immutable judgment, petitioners could not demonstrate the requisite grave abuse to justify intervention or stay; the finality of the CA ruling therefore decisively undercut their efforts to stave off execution.
</p>
</details>

## The decision mentions "grounds for relaxation" of the immutability doctrine and "exceptions" to the rule. What are they as listed in the opinion?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court acknowledged that the immutability doctrine is not absolute and that there are recognized grounds for relaxation as well as established exceptions. The grounds for relaxation enumerated in the opinion include: (a) matters of life, liberty, honor, or property; (b) existence of special or compelling circumstances; (c) the merits of the case; (d) a cause not entirely attributable to the fault or negligence of the party favored by suspension of the rules; (e) the lack of any showing that the review sought is merely frivolous and dilatory; and (f) that the other party will not be unjustly prejudiced thereby. The stated exceptions to immutability are: (i) correction of clerical errors; (ii) presence of nunc pro tunc entries which cause no prejudice; (iii) void judgments; and (iv) circumstances transpiring after finality that render execution unjust and inequitable. In this case, the Court concluded that none of these grounds or exceptions applied to justify altering the CA's final judgment.
</p>
</details>

## Why did the Supreme Court find that none of the grounds for relaxation or exceptions to immutability applied in this case?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court examined the record and concluded that petitioners failed to demonstrate any of the special circumstances or exceptions that would warrant relaxation of finality. There was no showing that the case involved issues of life, liberty, honor, or property in a way that would overcome finality; no special or compelling circumstances were established; the petitioners did not show that any post-finality events made execution unjust or inequitable; nor was there any clerical error or void judgment that could be remedied under the exceptions. The Court underscored that the CA ruling had been rendered after due process and had been properly enforced, and petitioners’ allegations did not amount to the extraordinary showing required to upset the immutability of a final appellate judgment. Therefore, none of the enumerated grounds for relaxation or exceptions applied to reverse or halt execution in this instance.
</p>
</details>

## What authoritative precedent did the Supreme Court cite to explain that the issuance of a writ of execution upon a final judgment is ministerial?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court cited Mauleon v. Porter, where it was explained that once a judgment attains finality and executory status, it becomes immutable and execution becomes a matter of right. The Mauleon decision underscored that implementation of a final judgment is ministerial on the courts; the prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to a writ of execution, and the trial court's issuance of that writ is a ministerial duty. The Supreme Court in the present case relied on Mauleon to support the conclusion that the RTC's compliance with the final appellate ruling by issuing the writ of execution did not constitute a grave abuse of discretion.
</p>
</details>

## After considering the legal standards, what disposition did the Supreme Court make of the two petitions and the lower-court rulings?

The Supreme Court made the following dispositions: (a) It dismissed the petition in G.R. No. 205074 for being moot and academic, since the main case before the Court of Appeals had already been resolved on the merits; and (b) it denied the petition in G.R. No. 231518 for lack of merit, finding that the RTC did not commit grave abuse of discretion in issuing the writ of execution and that the Court of Appeals correctly affirmed the RTC's orders. Consequently, the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' Decision dated June 22, 2016 and its Resolution dated March 23, 2017 in CA‑G.R. SP No. 03631‑MIN.
</p>
</details>

## Given the Court's rulings and reasons, why was a Rule 65 petition inappropriate to challenge the RTC's execution of a final appellate decision in this instance?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
A Rule 65 petition for certiorari is reserved for truly extraordinary circumstances where a lower court's action is patently and grossly beyond its jurisdiction or is a capricious exercise of power. Here, the RTC's order issuing a writ of execution was taken to implement a final and executory Court of Appeals judgment—an action that was ministerial and compelled by Rule 39, Section 1. Petitioners did not make the stringent showing of patent and gross abuse required to invoke certiorari, nor did they demonstrate that the RTC's actions were wholly void. Since execution on a final judgment is an established right of the prevailing party and a ministerial duty of the court, the Rule 65 remedy was not the appropriate vehicle to stay or reconsider such enforcement absent circumstances showing grave abuse or the applicability of recognized exceptions to the doctrine of immutability—circumstances the petitioners failed to establish.
</p>
</details>

## Analyze the petitioners' strategic choice to await appellate finality before seeking relief — how did timing affect their legal position based on the record?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The petitioners’ strategic posture, as reflected in the proceedings, shows that significant time elapsed between the RTC decision (August 31, 2006), the Court of Appeals' affirmation (September 25, 2008), denial of reconsideration (April 3, 2009), and the motion for execution and writ issuance in 2009. Petitioners waited until after the appellate ruling became final to mount collateral or extraordinary reliefs such as a Rule 65 petition seeking injunctive orders. Timing was decisive: because the CA decision became final and executory, respondent's motion for execution was legally enforceable and the RTC's issuance of the writ was ministerial and proper. By contesting execution only after finality, petitioners faced the high hurdle of demonstrating grounds to relax immutability or show grave abuse—both of which the record does not establish. If petitioners had timely preserved and pursued appellate remedies or raised substantive defenses before finality, their legal arguments might have been assessed in a different procedural posture. As it stands, their delayed strategy left them attempting to overturn or stay an immutable appellate judgment, which the courts uniformly rejected.
</p>
</details>

## What policy rationales did the Court rely upon to uphold the doctrine of finality and the ministerial duty to issue a writ of execution?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Court emphasized public policy considerations underlying the doctrine of immutability: (1) to avoid delay in the administration of justice and to make the discharge of judicial business orderly; and (2) to put an end to judicial controversies, thereby ensuring that parties’ rights and obligations do not remain indefinitely unresolved. The Court noted that, at the risk of occasional errors, finality fosters certainty and the enforceability of judgments. Additionally, the Court stressed that once a judgment is final the prevailing party should not be deprived of enforcement through subterfuge, because unjustified delay in enforcement undermines the courts’ role in disposing of controversies finally. These practical policy considerations supported the view that issuance of a writ of execution on a final judgment is a ministerial duty and should not be obstructed without extraordinary justification.
</p>
</details>

## Did the Supreme Court suggest any circumstance under which a final judgment may be revisited? If so, explain briefly as used in the decision.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Yes — the Supreme Court acknowledged that the immutability doctrine is not absolute and enumerated circumstances under which final judgments may be revisited or the rule relaxed. The opinion listed grounds for relaxation such as matters involving life, liberty, honor, or property; the existence of special or compelling circumstances; the merits of the case; situations not entirely attributable to the fault of the favored party; absence of a showing of frivolous or dilatory conduct; and lack of unjust prejudice to the other party. It also listed exceptions like clerical error correction, nunc pro tunc entries, void judgments, and post-finality circumstances rendering execution unjust or inequitable. However, the Court found that none of these grounds or exceptions applied to justify revisiting the final CA judgment in this case, hence no relaxation was warranted.
</p>
</details>

## If you were to explain to a client what this decision teaches about seeking injunctive relief pending execution of an adverse final judgment, what would you tell them based on the case?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
I would advise a client that once an appellate judgment becomes final and executory, enforcement by the prevailing party is a right, and the trial court's issuance of a writ of execution is generally ministerial. Seeking a TRO or writ of preliminary injunction to stay execution after finality requires an extraordinary showing: the petitioner must demonstrate grave abuse of discretion by the implementing court, or that recognized grounds or exceptions to finality apply (such as clerical error, voidness, or post-finality circumstances making execution unjust). Merely arguing disagreement with the judgment, filing collateral remedies late, or asserting theoretical hardships will not suffice. Thus, in practice, one must be timely in pursuing available appeals and prepare compelling, fact-specific grounds if attempting to impede execution once a judgment is final.
</p>
</details>

## On what grounds did the Supreme Court reject petitioners' contention that the two-year period should be reckoned from the CA's finality date?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The petitioners argued that the two-year period fixed by the RTC should start from the finality of the CA ruling (identified in the record as October 25, 2008). The Supreme Court rejected this contention effectively by highlighting that the Court of Appeals itself had determined that the two-year period had already lapsed as reckoned from the trial court's decision; the CA considered the RTC's fixation and concluded the lease had already terminated. Moreover, because the CA judgment became final and executory and was immutable, the subsequent enforcement through writ of execution was proper. Petitioners' attempt to re-litigate the timing of the two‑year period after finality did not meet the standards to displace a final appellate determination, so the Court found no basis to halt execution on that theory.
</p>
</details>

## How does this case illustrate the interplay between substantive resolution of disputes (e.g., lease period, rent) and procedural bars to relief (finality, execution)?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The case demonstrates that even when parties contest substantive matters—here, the reasonable period of lease and payment for improvements—procedural finality can be dispositive. The RTC resolved the substantive dispute by fixing a lease period and rent; the CA affirmed and found the period had lapsed; upon finality, respondent was entitled to execution. Procedural doctrines—immutability of final judgments, the ministerial nature of execution, and the limited scope of Rule 65—prevent parties from re-opening finalized substantive determinations except in narrowly defined circumstances. Thus, litigants must secure substantive relief through timely and proper appellate channels before finality, because otherwise the procedural consequences of final judgments (execution, loss of possession) will take effect despite unresolved contentions like counterclaims for improvements.
</p>
</details>

## What lessons for practitioners in landlord-tenant or lease disputes can be distilled from the Court's decision?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
Several practical lessons emerge: (1) Timeliness is critical—parties must raise and pursue appeals and preserve issues prior to finality, as final judgments are generally immutable. (2) If an appellate decision is imminent or has become final, seeking injunctive relief to prevent execution requires a stringent showing—mere disagreement or delay tactics will not suffice. (3) When a tenant claims investments or improvements, the claimant should aggressively litigate and secure substantive remedies in the primary proceedings rather than rely on post-finality collateral remedies. (4) Counsel should anticipate that enforcement of a final judgment is a matter of right for the prevailing party and prepare appropriate defenses or negotiate settlements before execution procedures commence. These lessons emphasize procedural strategy as much as substantive legal arguments.
</p>
</details>

## Critically assess whether petitioners met their burden to show that the RTC's issuance of the writ of execution was a "capricious or whimsical exercise of judgment."

<details>

<p>

## What is the final practical instruction from the Supreme Court to lower courts when confronted with a motion to execute a final appellate judgment?

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
The Supreme Court's instruction is clear: when a judgment has become final and executory, a motion for execution must be granted as a matter of right and the issuance of a writ of execution is a ministerial duty. Lower courts should not refuse to execute an unappealed and final judgment absent a manifest ground to relax finality or an applicable exception (e.g., clerical error, voidness, or other extraordinary post-finality circumstances). In short, trial courts must implement final appellate rulings promptly and are constrained from exercising discretion to withhold execution except in extraordinary and legally justified situations.
</p>

## Summarize, in one paragraph, the core significance of this decision for law students preparing for oral recitation.

<details>
<summary><em>Show Answer</em></summary>
<p>
This decision underscores two central doctrines: (1) the immutability of final judgments and the ministerial duty of courts to enforce them—with execution upon a final judgment being a matter of right under Rule 39, Section 1—and (2) the narrow scope of Rule 65 certiorari to correct only patent and gross abuses of discretion that amount to lack of jurisdiction. For an oral recitation, students should be able to narrate the factual chronology (lease arrangement at ₱100, RTC fixing of two-year period, CA finding that the term had lapsed, execution proceedings, Rule 65 petitions), explain why the petition attacking an interlocutory denial of TRO/WPI was moot, apply the high standard for "grave abuse of discretion," and articulate why none of the exceptions to finality applied. The ruling teaches the primacy of finality and procedural strategy: substantive claims must be timely litigated before finality because post-finality remedies are tightly circumscribed.
</p>
